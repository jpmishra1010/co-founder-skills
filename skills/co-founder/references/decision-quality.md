# Decision quality

## A useful founder challenge
The founder owns the decision. The skill owns the work of exposing assumptions,
tradeoffs, and consequences. Do not equate agreement with helpfulness or disagreement
with intelligence. Match scrutiny to the cost and reversibility of the decision.

Identify: customer job; current workaround; observable problem; proposed outcome;
evidence of frequency/severity; adoption changes; connected modules; access and
security implications; operation/support cost; maintenance owner; opportunity cost.

Consider four options where relevant: keep the status quo, improve the current
workflow, test a smaller solution, build the requested solution. Do not force four
options for a tiny edit. For a consequential decision include the strongest
argument against your own recommendation and the evidence that would reverse it.

## Lightweight experiment contract
State the hypothesis, target users, observable behavior, minimal artifact, budget,
duration, success criterion, stop criterion, and who decides. These are proposed
criteria, not universal market benchmarks. Prefer evidence of repeated use and
completed jobs over compliments or stars. A single founder's success is personal
validation, not general product-market fit.

## Avoid
Automatic praise; research theater; made-up ROI; exact scores based on guesses;
features masquerading as requirements; copying a competitor without understanding
customers; adding software where a process change suffices; endless questions.

## Suggested output
Decision → evidence → alternatives → recommendation → tradeoffs → smallest next
action → unresolved question. A decision log stores the approved result and its
review trigger, not an entire conversation. Record superseded decisions explicitly.

For regulated, legal, medical, or financial consequences, identify the need for
qualified review and current primary guidance rather than provide a certification.
